# One recommendation — Nepal

Participant: Suwarna Pyakurel (solo). Research date: 16 October 2026.

## Action: Modification

Amend **Schedule 1 of Nepal's Customs Tariff Act, 2081 (2024), as subsequently amended**, to cap the standard non-preferential **ad valorem basic customs duty on goods in HS chapters 25–97 at 20%**, retaining any currently lower rate. Legislate the ceiling's introduction over the next three annual budgets. This is one tariff-schedule modification, not a package of additional policies.

Authority: the Ministry of Finance should propose the statutory schedule changes through the annual Finance Bill; the Federal Parliament enacts the amendment and the Department of Customs administers it. This proposal changes basic customs duties; it does not propose amendments to VAT, excise, other charges, import prohibitions, or tariff preferences. Specific/mixed duties are outside the proposed ad valorem ceiling and would remain a limitation of its coverage.

**The 20% ceiling and three-budget transition are proposed design choices, not estimates of an optimal rate or implementation period from our data.** No Nepal income gain, revenue effect, pass-through rate, employment effect or number of affected tariff lines is estimated. An implementation decision would need fiscal and product-level appraisal; the present submission supplies historical descriptive evidence and a liberty rationale, not that appraisal.

## Mechanism, evidence and limits

Higher import duties constrain voluntary exchange by raising the tax wedge facing purchasers. A lower ceiling can reduce costs on affected imports and increase competitive pressure and choice. Firms using affected inputs could face lower costs. Pass-through and adjustment depend on market structure and other taxes; the required datasets do not directly test those mechanisms.

Figures 1–2 establish that the studied countries experienced policy changes, using raw tariffs and the chain-linked institutional context separately. Figures 4–6 show heterogeneous income outcomes, not a reliable estimated tariff premium. Figure 7 shows sensitivity to dating. Figure 8 establishes that Nepal starts from a lower historical mean than several large cutters; it does **not** estimate the benefit of the proposed ceiling.

The data support investigating a limited, transparent reduction in remaining tariff peaks more readily than promising that a very large mean-tariff cut will reproduce China’s growth. The recommendation is a normative judgement informed by those limits; the historical comparison alone does not establish that 20% is best or that any gain will occur.

## Strongest objection and liberty tradeoff

Tariff revenue could fall before other revenues adjust, potentially putting public services under pressure; protected firms and workers may also bear concentrated adjustment losses. The project does not quantify those costs. A three-budget transition changes timing, not the existence of that tradeoff.

The liberty argument favours predictable rules and wider freedom for purchasers and sellers to exchange, rather than protecting selected producers at the expense of diffuse buyers. That principle does not make the fiscal and worker costs disappear. The modest and mixed comparative evidence must remain visible alongside the recommendation.
